Communications
by lunar location

Near the south pole, lighting and the view of Earth vary with terrain and date. These five examples illustrate the trade-offs to compare.

The five locations

NEAR SIDE · EQUATORIAL REGION

Tranquility Base

Apollo 11's landing site in the Sea of Tranquility shows that direct communication is possible from the near side. Earth is much higher in the sky there than at the south pole.

What this means for a mission

Key point: a clear horizon helps, but antennas, power and ground stations are still essential.

Apollo 11 communications · NASA ↗

SOUTH POLE · HIGH GROUND

Shackleton's ridges

Some high ground around this polar crater receives abundant sunlight. That makes it attractive for solar power, but does not guarantee a continuous view of Earth, low on the horizon.

What this means for a mission

Check: the exact ridge, antenna height and date; two nearby points can have different horizons.

Shackleton's terrain · NASA ↗

SOUTH POLE · CRATER INTERIOR

Inside Shackleton

Its permanently shadowed areas are of interest in the search for volatile substances. However, the crater walls can block Earth and even a relay on a nearby ridge.

What this means for a mission

Plan for: a verified radio path to the rover, energy reserves and an operating mode for outages. Solar shadow and radio blockage are separate issues.

Shackleton relay study · NASA ↗

FAR SIDE · SOUTH POLE–AITKEN BASIN

Schrödinger basin

This large impact basin offers insights into the Moon's geological history. On the far side, it has no direct view of Earth: increasing transmitter power does not remove the obstacle.

What this means for a mission

Plan for: a relay with paths to both the surface and Earth, coverage windows and data storage where needed.

Schrödinger basin · NASA ↗

Apollo 15 landing site
